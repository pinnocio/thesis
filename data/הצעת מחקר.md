**Introduction**

A prominent perspective in social science is to view the nation not as a fixed entity but a dynamic social and cultural construct, continuously debated, challenged, and redefined by ongoing struggles over its meaning, core values, and criteria for belonging, which often revolve around the drawing and contestation of boundaries (Brubaker, 2009; Lamont & Molnár, 2002). In times of national turmoil, in what Feinstein defines as “Nation-Disrupting-Events” (Feinstein, 2020; 2024), struggles over the nation and the national identity intensify, bringing old and new societal divisions to the forefront, sharpening fundamental debates about who belongs to the nation and what it fundamentally represents. In such events, nationalism provides essential frameworks for understanding collective identity and the political community, offering the shared language, symbols, and narratives through which these boundaries and meanings are articulated and contested (Bonikowski & DiMaggio, 2016; Calhoun, 1993; Greenfeld, 2001; Wimmer, 2013). Feinstein’s (2023) empirical study offers a valuable analytical framework for grasping these dynamics by identifying five distinct nationalism types within the Jewish-Israeli population, each characterized by a unique configuration of beliefs and attitudes regarding the national identity of the state of Israel.

The Israel-Hamas war, which began on October 7th, 2023, is a nation-disrupting event that has brought significant ethical and social dilemmas to the forefront of the national public sphere. The extensive public debates on military operations, civilian casualties, and hostage negotiations reveal a variety of approaches to addressing the moral and social issues of the war, reflecting the nation’s existing cleavages, political stances, and historical divisions and ideologies. This study is interested in how these challenging issues are morally framed and justified within public discourse, a process by which social actors strategically employ moral language, principles, and narratives to define and interpret situations (Hitlin & Vaisey, 2013; Rozin, 1997; Spillman, 2023). Analyzing such framings offers valuable insights into how different national perceptions and beliefs are revealed and expressed during times of crisis. For this analysis, Feinstein’s (2023) typology provides a useful framework. Together with literature on moral framing, moral justification, moralization and moral conviction, it offers distinct lenses to examine how various nationalism types, each with their own beliefs about the nation, might use specific moral justifications and narratives in these debates. Ultimately, exploring how these challenging issues are framed and discussed in public discourse aims to enrich the existing literature and deepen our understanding of Jewish-Israeli society and the ongoing conflict between Israel and the Palestinians.

Employing qualitative content analysis of online opinion articles, this study aims to investigate the interplay between nationalism – broadly understood as the ideas, sentiments, and practices associated with the nation and national identity (Anderson, 2006; Bonikowski & DiMaggio, 2016; Brubaker, 1996; Greenfeld, 2001\) – and moral reasoning during the Israel-Hamas war. The primary goal is to understand how different nationalism types within Jewish-Israeli society interpret, frame, and morally justify key issues related to the conflict in public discourse. The research question guiding this study is: **How do different nationalism types within Jewish-Israeli society, as expressed in online opinion articles, morally and emotionally frame, discuss, and justify their positions on key ethical dilemmas arising from the Israel-Hamas war?**

**Literature Review**

**1\.**

Sociologists and political scientists often view the social world as characterized by ongoing struggles over meanings, resources, and power that take place within various social contexts and institutions. These struggles frequently take shape through the drawing and contestation of symbolic boundaries – conceptual distinctions made by social actors for categorizing and defining group membership, playing an important role in the creation of inequality and the exercise of power (Lamont & Molnár, 2002; Edgell et al., 2020). Within this dynamic, the nation stands out as a particularly significant arena of contestation. In the national sphere, different actors actively compete to define or shape key elements of collective life, such as who legitimately belongs, which core values the nation upholds, the historical narratives it embraces, and its role in the global order (Abowitz & Harnish, 2006; Brubaker, 2009; Feinstein, 2024). Consequently, how the nation and its boundaries are defined becomes central to political power struggles and the allocation of rights and privileges (Brubaker, 2009; Wimmer, 2002). In these struggles over the nation’s meaning and boundaries, nationalism plays a crucial role.

Nationalism is broadly understood as the set of ideas, sentiments, and representations through which "the people" make sense of their nation and their place within it (Bonikowski & DiMaggio, 2016; Greenfeld, 2001). As a powerful social and cultural construct (Anderson, 2006; Brubaker, 1996), it provides foundational categories and shared understandings (Strauss & Quinn, 1997\) that continually shape, debate, and challenge national identities (Feinstein, 2024; פיינשטיין, 2023). This process influences how individuals categorize themselves and others, interpret historical and contemporary events, and understand shared cultural and national symbols, norms and values, fostering a distinctive form of collective identity, ultimately defining what constitutes a "nation" (Calhoun, 1993; Greenfeld, 2001; Ram, 2011). In this sense, nationalism not only provides the framework for these interpretive acts (Feinstein, 2024\) but also cultivates a sense of belonging to large-scale communities, where national identities often "trump" other personal or group identities and link individuals directly to the nation-as-a-whole (Abowitz & Harnish, 2006; Calhoun, 1993). This cognitive dimension of nationalism allows for the emotional (Feinstein, 2024; Lomsky-Feder, 2011\) and moral (Hutchinson, 2017; Brubaker, 2009\) investments individuals make in their national communities, making it an important lens for understanding how nations are constructed and politically mobilized (Anderson, 2006; Smith, 1991). 

Citizenship is a formal membership within a political community that defines the specific rights, duties, and avenues for participation available to individuals (Ben-Porat & Turner, 2011). Describe as the “right to have rights” (Arendt, 1976, cited in Ben-Porat & Turner, 2011), this status highlights its role as the basis for individuals to claim access to state protections and resources. As such, the criteria embedded in citizenship laws become central to ongoing struggles over national identity and the nation’s character (פיינשטיין, 2023). Thus, citizenship laws are more than administrative rules; they function as powerful tools of social closure such as providing or denying opportunities (Brubaker, 2009), drawing formal boundaries between insiders and outsiders and actively shaping the nation’s limits, both between states and within them (Wimmer, 2013). However, focusing solely on the legal or functional aspects of citizenship risks overlooking the symbolic and moral dimensions deeply intertwined with nationhood and national identity (Brubaker, 2009). This complexity arises because citizenship is actively shaped and contested through different citizenship discourses, each representing distinct frameworks for understanding membership, identity, values, and participation (Abowitz & Harnish, 2006; Shafir & Peled, 2002).

Citizenship discourses function as the primary means through which the meaning of citizenship is created, expressed, and contested (Abowitz & Harnish, 2006; Shafir & Peled, 2002). Building on this perspective, Shafir and Peled (2002) distinguish three principal "citizenship discourses," each offering a distinct framework for understanding membership, rights, and duties within a nation-state. The first, liberal discourse, focuses on personal liberty and private property, casting individuals as the main holders of universal, equal, and publicly recognized rights (Abowitz and Harnish, 2006). In exchange, citizens are expected to meet only minimal obligations, such as obeying the law, paying taxes, voting, and serving in the military (Shafir & Peled, 2002). This discourse supports a self-restrained political domain that encourages religious, cultural, and political pluralism by staying neutral with respect to different conceptions of the good (Abowitz and Harnish, 2006; Shafir & Peled, 2002). The second discourse, civic republicanism, connects citizenship rights to active participation and contributions to the communal good, placing high value on civic virtue and engagement (Abowitz and Harnish, 2006). This often introduces a hierarchy based on levels of contribution to the community (Shafir & Peled, 2002). The third, ethno-nationalism, defines citizenship primarily as membership in a unified descent group or “members of an ethnic community” (Wimmer, 2013), with the state represents the nation’s particular cultural traits such as language, religion, and history, generally resisting cultural assimilation (Shafir & Peled, 2002). 

These citizenship discourses, each emphasizing either individual rights, collective participation, or shared descent/ethnicity, are fundamental to shaping a society's incorporation regime (Shafir & Peled, 2002\) in determining how rights, responsibilities, and privileges are distributed among different groups, legitimizing the allocation of resources and opportunities for both insiders and outsider (Brubaker, 2009; Shafir & Peled, 2002). As a result, proposed revisions to citizenship criteria usually provoke broad, ideologically charged debates about legitimate belonging and the core identity of the nation (Ben-Porat & Turner, 2011; Brubaker, 2009; פיינשטיין, 2023). Understanding these conceptual models is therefore crucial for examining how national identities are constructed and contested in diverse political contexts.

**2\.**

Jewish-Israeli society is shaped by an interplay of national, cultural, socioeconomic, ethnic, and religious influences, resulting in a highly fragmented political landscape (פרס & בן-רפאל, 2006). Central to Israel’s political and social life are ongoing debates over national identity and the criteria for group belonging, which reflect fundamental disagreements about what defines the nation (Ram, 2019; Shapira, 2018). For example, the persistent tension between Jewish VS Israeli identity (Shavit & Shavit, 2016), as does the ongoing contestation over how rights and resources should be distributed (Ben-Porat & Turner, 2011). Key societal divisions include differing attitudes toward Israeli Arabs and Palestinians, shaped by the ongoing Arab-Israeli conflict. These divisions place security concerns, ethnic and national sentiments, and the centrality of military strength at the heart of the national identity (Levy, 2022). In addition to these divides, there are significant tensions between secular and religious Jews, particularly regarding the role of religion in public life and its relationship to the state. Another longstanding cleavage exists between Mizrahi and Ashkenazi Jews, rooted in a history of systematic marginalization (Illouz, 2023; פרס & בן-רפאל, 2006). Understanding these enduring cleavages is essential for grasping the complexity of contemporary Israeli society, particularly during periods of acute internal crisis or in times of military conflict.

Israel's political landscape and its incorporation regime are shaped by the dynamic interaction of three citizenship discourses: civic republican, Jewish ethno-national, and liberal (Shafir & Peled, 2002). Civic republicanism played a dominant role in Israel’s pre-state era and its first decades as a state. At first, grounding Zionism’s legitimacy in historical Jewish rights, and the efforts of “pioneering” (chalutziyu), such as labour, and military service. As it developed, the focus shifted more toward state authority and a shared commitment to national goals (mamlachtiyut), still rooted in Zionist values. This approach led to a stratified society (turned citizenship with statehood), where access to rights and privileges was mostly tied to contributions towards the Zionist project, affecting both Jewish and Arab citizens, as well as different Jewish subgroups (pp. 17-19). At the same time, Jewish ethno-national discourse defined citizenship primarily as membership in and belonging to the Jewish people, granting rights simply for being Jewish through mechanisms like the Law of Return and seeing the state as representing the Jewish people as a whole. This discourse was often used, sometimes even under the guise of republican discourse, to justify discrimination between Jewish and Palestinian citizens within Israel's sovereign borders and over time, has become increasingly infused with religious content (p. 22).

The Liberal discourse, which emphasizes individual rights, began to take on a more prominent role in Israel from the mid-1980s. (Shafir & Peled, 2002, p. 19). While, in principle, designed to be inclusive, its practical implementation in Israel is frequently tied to market participation, making individuals and groups with historically greater access to market resources more likely to benefit, thus perpetuating existing inequalities, what Shafir and Peled call “contra-democratic effects” (p. 21). Historically, the civic republican discourse served as a mediating role between the exclusionary tendencies of ethno-nationalism and the more inclusive goals of liberalism (p. 338). However, in the late 70s, its decline has resulted in a far more direct divide between liberal and Jewish ethno-national discourses, contributing to increased political fragmentation and a widespread sense of “crisis” in Israeli politics.

**3\.**

The Israel-Hamas war that began on October 7th, 2023, stands out as a "nation-disrupting event." (Feinstein, 2020; Feinstein, 2024). Such events are characterized by a widespread perception among the public that fundamental elements of the collective national identity are at stake, thereby breaking the illusion of a unified collective identity and evoking a deep sense of insecurity. In such moments of conflict, the political arena becomes a symbolic battleground where different groups compete to define the crisis’s meaning, interpret its implications for national identity, and shape the collective response (Feinstein, 2024; פיינשטיין, 2023). Scholarship on nationhood during crises highlights how such moments not only intensify nationalist sentiments but also expose and deepen internal divisions (Bonikowski & DiMaggio, 2016). The Israel-Hamas war has brought both new dilemmas and long-standing issues to the forefront of public discourse, intensifying existing internal divisions (פרס & בן-רפאל, 2006), and raising fundamental questions about collective identity, solidarity, and the future of the “Jewish nation” (בלאנדר, 2024; Cohen, 2024). The ongoing conflict raised difficult questions and heated debates concerning the prioritization of military objectives versus the return of hostages, as well as the value placed on human life – both Israeli and Palestinian –  raising ethical and moral concerns about the state’s role, collective social and moral responsibilities, and strategic priorities (Hermann et al., 2025; Kasher, 2024; Navot & Paz, 2024). 

**Theoretical Frameworks**

Feinstein's (2023) study provides an analytical framework for understanding the diverse national sentiments within the Jewish-Israeli population. Building on Shafir and Peled’s three discourses, it differentiates between several nuanced forms of ethno-nationalism, allowing a nuanced look into Shafir and Peled's broader "ethno-national" category, allowing for a more granular, in-depth analysis.  This framework is based on survey data analysed using Latent Class Analysis (LCA), a quantitative technique for identifying subgroups formed by shared patterns of responses (Weller et al., 2020). Feinstein’s qualitative interpretation of these statistical groupings led to the identification of five types of nationalism: Ultra-nationalism, Ethno-religious nationalism, Ethno-republican nationalism, Ethnocentric nationalism, and Critical nationalism. Each type is characterized by its unique profile across six dimensions, each represented by specific survey items. These dimensions are: strength of national identification (e.g., emotional closeness to Israel); belief in collective victimhood (e.g., perceptions of historical Jewish suffering); perception of cultural uniqueness (e.g., beliefs about Jewish heritage or a special role); level of state chauvinism (e.g., supporting Israel unconditionally or viewing Israel as superior); forms of national pride (e.g., pride in the IDF or scientific achievements); and criteria for inclusion in the national collective (e.g., the importance of being Jewish versus respecting political institutions for “true Israeli” status). The combination and intensity of attitudes across these six dimensions define each nationalism type (פיינשטיין, 2023, p. 149). 

These five types, grounded in both empirical data and qualitative analysis, reflect a range of cognitive (Rawlings & Childress, 2021), cultural (DiMaggio, 1997; Strauss & Quinn, 1997), and moral (Jasper, 2023, discussing Michèle Lamont) frameworks through which individuals interpret the nation and its boundaries. Together, they capture a spectrum of national sentiments that shape how people come to understand and experience the nation as a shared entity. Contemporary sociology of morality research emphasizes that these frameworks are not static or universal but rather are shaped by shared understanding and meanings that emerge within specific cultural contexts (Hitlin & Vaisey, 2013; Spillman, 2023). Rather than being abstract or universal, these shared frameworks are dynamic, socially constructed set of norms, values, and expectations (Abbott, 2020). Specifically, moral frameworks include both explicit and implicit norms that guide people’s judgments of what is appropriate, admirable, or shameful for themselves and others (Hitlin, 2014), influencing not only individual behaviour but also the emotional and relational dynamics of daily social interaction (Jasper, 2023; Polletta, 2023). Contemporary scholarship on the sociology of morality (Hitlin & Vaisey, 2013; Spillman, 2023\) expands this perspective to include narratives, identities, institutions, symbolic boundaries, and cognitive schemas, viewing morality as embedded within overlapping social groups and identities, and as subject to ongoing negotiation, contestation, and exclusion.

Within this broader framework, two concepts are especially useful for analysis: moralization and moral conviction. Moralization describes the process by which certain activities, behaviours, or objects are assigned a moral dimension and reframed as inherently right or wrong (Rozin, 1997; 1999). This process becomes visible not only through the use of moral language (obligation, duty, virtue, or wrongdoing), but also through explicit concern for others’ behaviour and the expectation that others should share these moral standards (Rozin, 1997; Lynch, 2014). As attitudes and practices become deeply moralized, they are internalized and woven into both individual and collective identities, making them particularly enduring and resistant to change (Rozin, 1997; 1999). Emotional reactions, especially disgust and outrage, are central to moralization, often serving as powerful signals that a particular issue or behaviour has become morally charged (Clifford, 2019; Rozin, 1997). Moralization often “piggybacks” on established moral principles or expands to create new ones, providing justification for actions and social arrangements that might otherwise be seen as intrusive or controversial by framing them as morally necessary (Lynch, 2014; Rozin, 1997; Wise et al., 2023). Through this process, sharp symbolic and social boundaries are drawn between what is considered pure or polluted, worthy or unworthy, and between those who are regarded as credible or suspect (Hitlin & Vaisey, 2013; Lamont et al., 2015). In this way, moralization not only guides individual behaviour and social participation but also acts as a powerful tool for defining who and what is viewed as morally acceptable within a given community (Hitlin, 2014; Rozin, 1999; Spillman, 2023).

Closely related, moral conviction refers to the strength and intensity with which moralized attitudes are held, elevating certain positions to the status of non-negotiable moral imperatives (Clifford, 2019). When individuals view an issue through the lens of moral conviction, it is experienced as a matter of fundamental right and wrong, deeply anchored in core values and accompanied by strong emotions – particularly anger and disgust (Clifford, 2019; Rozin, 1997; Wisneski & Skitka, 2017). These powerful feelings are not incidental; they signal the depth of commitment and often arise in response to perceived harm, injustice, or violations of deeply held principles (Clifford, 2019). Disgust, in particular, has a unique ability to transform personal preferences into moral imperatives, making compromise or negotiation virtually unthinkable (Rozin, 1999). This resistance to compromise frequently manifests as lower tolerance for disagreement, greater social distance from those with opposing views, and an increased willingness to morally condemn or exclude dissenters (Clifford, 2019). Moments of “moral shock” (Jasper & Poulsen 1995\) – highly emotive or morally charged events – can further solidify and intensify these convictions (Wisneski & Skitka, 2017). Together, these concepts play a pivotal role in shaping boundaries of belonging and exclusion, especially when collective identities are at stake, and helps explain why moral frameworks often mobilize both intense solidarity and deep societal conflict (Clifford, 2019; Lynch, 2014; Yazdiha, 2023). 

The ongoing Israel-Hamas war has significantly intensified discourse and debates surrounding several critical issues. These include ethical challenges and considerations associated with military operations, the complex and intricate dilemmas concerning the hostages held in Gaza and the urgent need for negotiated solutions, along with the associated terms and costs and the value of civilian life – both Israeli and Palestinian. This study aims to examine these issues through the theoretical frameworks discussed earlier, offering insight into how different national perspectives shape collective judgments of right and wrong during times of acute crisis. 

**Research Questions**

This study aims to uncover the deep, underlying cultural logics guiding ongoing debates within Jewish-Israeli society surrounding the Israel-Hamas war, particularly heightened during the intense and emotional week of the second hostage release deal. The premise of this study is that these public debates, marked by a spectrum of moral and emotional narratives, are not merely a clash of differing opinions. Rather, they are part of a deeper cultural reality, structured by different interpretive frameworks. These frameworks provide the moral and emotional schemas through which different groups within Jewish-Israeli society construct, contest, articulate, and justify their positions on social, political, and moral dilemmas, especially in times of war. This premise leads to the following primary research question and its guiding sub-questions:

**Primary Question:** 

How do different nationalism types, as expressed in online opinion articles, morally and emotionally frame, debate, and justify their positions on key ethical dilemmas arising from the Israel-Hamas war?

**Sub questions:**

1. How do the nationalism types differ in their positions on key ethical dilemmas in regard to Israel-Hamas war and the Hostage release.  
2. What specific moral and emotional frameworks are characteristic of each nationalism type?

To explore these research questions, this study will employ a qualitative content analysis of online opinion articles using Feinstein’s (2023) typology of nationalism types as its central analytical tool. This typology has already proven effective in revealing the deeper logics guiding public debate behind the 2023 judicial reform. By systematically mapping these elements, this research seeks to shed light on more framing, reasoning and justifications and the emotional expressions and language in times of national crisis. In doing so, the study will also enrich our understanding of the different logics encompassing the Israeli-Jewish society, especially within the broader context of the Israel-Palestine conflict.

**Methodology**

This study employs qualitative content analysis to examine differences in moral interpretations and justifications among distinct “nationalism types,” as expressed within published opinion articles regarding the Israel-Hamas war. The primary unit of analysis is each opinion article’s text, and not its author.  This decision is based on an increasing understanding in cultural sociology that people often rely on different, context-specific cultural logics, instead of a stable or consistent view. However, while individuals might hold complex or changing context-dependant views, a published opinion article usually offers a more coherent and focused version. Thus, analysing the text itself, we can engage more directly with the arguments and narratives as they are presented in public discourse.

Although Feinstein's (2023) typology is quantitative, many survey items 'העולם היה מקום טוב יותר אם אנשים במדינות אחרות היו דומים יותר לישראלים' or 'העולם היה מקום טוב יותר אם עמים אחרים היו מאמצים ערכים יהודיים', rely on subjective understandings and interpretations of 'ישראלי' or 'ערכים יהודיים'. Similarly, items like 'עד כמה אתה קרוב רגשית אל ישראל?' and 'עד כמה חשוב לך להרגיש ישראלי' depend on individual interpretations of national belonging, which are shaped by the complex national, cultural, ethnic, and religious influences within Jewish-Israeli society. This inherent subjectivity within the quantitative typology provides a solid rationale for using a qualitative approach to unpack these understandings as they manifest in public discourse. The study thus uses Feinstein's typology as a framework to categorize dominant nationalistic discursive patterns as identified in each article, analysing how textual expressions align with Feinstein’s nationalism types rather than inferring fixed or static individual identities.

The study focuses on opinion articles published in Israeli online newspapers between January 12-20, 2025\. This specific timeframe is intentionally chosen as it represents a “nation-disrupting event”, occurring after the initial stage of the second hostage release deal and during heightened anticipation for further releases. Public debate and emotions around this period were intensified, especially regarding tough ethical questions like balancing military goals against the return of hostages, and the broader discussion over the value of human life. Analysing discourse during this focused and charged period allows for an in-depth examination of the moral interpretations mobilized within the different nationalism types.

The corpus currently includes 180 online op-ed articles collected from a wide array of mainstream online newspapers such as Haaretz, ynet, ישראל היום, ערוץ 7, and בחדרי חרדים. The decision to focus exclusively on online articles is an intentional methodological choice, reflecting their role as immediate and widely accessed platforms for public discourse, where ideologies are articulated and public sentiment is both reflected and projected, and out of convenience. For the purpose of this study, “opinion articles” are defined as editorial pieces, opinion articles, or commentary (excluding news reports) that directly discuss issues regarding the on-going war as previously discussed. Inclusion criteria: 1\. The article is explicitly labelled or tagged as "דעה" or “פרשנות”. 2\. The author expresses his or her opinion, either explicitly or implicitly on matters such as the Hostages, ceasefire deal, the Israel-Hamas war and Israeli-Palestinian conflict, harm to Civilian casualties. Exclusion criteria: 1\. News reports. 2\. Multiple authors. 3\. Fringe newspapers. 4\. Articles that can’t be classified as having a dominant nationalism type (more than one).

The analytical framework for this study is based on Feinstein’s (2023) typology of five nationalism types, operationalized through six dimensions: strength of national identification, belief in collective victimhood, perception of cultural uniqueness, level of state chauvinism, specific forms of national pride, and criteria for inclusion within the national collective. Applying this typology, designed for quantitative application – to qualitative text-based content analysis presents a methodological consideration. Opinion articles may not explicitly articulate all dimensions with quantifiable intensity. However, as previously discussed, the inherent subjectivity of the survey items reflects a diverse and nuanced range of understandings, which further supports a qualitative approach to interpreting and accounting for the different meanings present within the texts. 

Building on these six dimensions, a coding scheme (Krippendorff, 2018\) will be constructed to identify the dominant nationalism type expressed in each article. To address the challenge of translating quantitative survey indicators into qualitative textual patterns, a demo textual analysis was conducted, and a test coding table was created (see Appendix). For example, strength of national identification might be reflected in explicit statements of loyalty or emotional attachment to the nation. Belief in collective victimhood could be identified through references to historical or contemporary shared experiences of suffering, persecution, or injustice (primarily towards Jews). Perception of cultural uniqueness involves claims or assertions about the distinctiveness or special character of the national culture or values. Level of state chauvinism might be captured through expressions of belief in the superiority of the Jewish people. Specific forms of national pride include expressions of pride related to various aspects of the nation, such as military achievements. Lastly, criteria for inclusion focus on statements defining who belongs to the nation, including references to ethnicity, religion, civic participation, or other markers. 

Following this categorization, the analysis will focus on the moral interpretations of conflict-related issues, highlighting the main ethical and social dilemmas discussed in the texts. These dilemmas include the tension between prioritizing the return of hostages and the continuation of military operations, ethical questions surrounding civilian casualties, and debates over the state’s moral responsibilities. The analysis will then systematically identify and characterize the different forms of moral discourse, symbols, and values employed, as well as the different narratives and justifications. Drawing on established sociological approaches to the study of morality, the analysis will delve into how the articles employ explicit moral language and appeals (e.g., right/wrong, duty, justice), express or evoke specific moral emotions (e.g., anger, disgust, pride), and utilize narratives of moral justification, such as arguments based on self-defence, collective victimhood, or national destiny. For example, the analysis will note instances where actions are framed as “sacred duties” or where opponents are framed as “morally corrupt,” both of which point to processes of moralization. It will also pay attention to expressions of moral conviction, such as uncompromising statements that deem negotiating with the enemy as inherently wrong or representing particular positions as clearly outside the bounds of legitimate debate. In addition, the analysis will investigate how symbolic moral boundaries are drawn to include or exclude and frame particular groups. 

The complexity of different moral concepts and frameworks adds further challenges. While the analysis seeks to identify core moral arguments and justifications, it may not capture every nuance. To address this, the study will draw on contemporary scholarly work on the sociology of morality (Hitlin & Vaisey, 2013), cultural and cognitive sociology, and systematically codes for markers of moralization (Rozin, 1997, 1999\) and moral conviction (Clifford, 2019; Wisneski & Skitka, 2017). Using established literature from both Sociology and Psychology allows for a more in-depth analysis and interpretation of moral frameworks. 

**Limitations**

This study is subject to several limitations stemming from its design. The narrow data corpus, consisting solely of online opinion articles published during a single intense week of the Israel Hamas war inherently restricts the ability to trace changes in moral and emotional sentiments over time or during other stages of the conflict. Furthermore, the decision to limit the analysis to newspaper opinion articles, while excluding other forms of public communication, each shaped by its own organizational and cultural characteristics, influencing the articulation of ideas and sentiments. Although the selected newspapers aim to reflect diverse political and ideological perspectives, they may not represent the full range of opinions within Jewish-Israeli society. The qualitative content analysis used in this study also has inherent limitations, as some subjectivity in coding and interpretation is unavoidable, even with a systematic coding scheme in place. Moreover, inferring “nationalism types” from opinion articles is limited by the definitions provided by Feinstein (2023), whose typology was derived from survey data on individual self-identification. As such, the present analysis does not attempt to categorize individual authors, but rather to identify the dominant nationalist discourse expressed within each article, recognizing that multiple discursive elements may coexist within a single text. Finally, as a case study centered on Jewish-Israeli society during a specific period of conflict, the generalizability of the findings is naturally limited. The political, cultural, and historical context of Israel, particularly during the Israel Hamas war, is unique, and the insights derived from this study are necessarily embedded in this particular context.
